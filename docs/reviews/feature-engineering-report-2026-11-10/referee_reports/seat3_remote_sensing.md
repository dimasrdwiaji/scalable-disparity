# Referee Report: Spatial Feature Engineering for Physical Disparity
**Reviewer Identity / Seat**: Remote Sensing & Environmental Injustice
**Evaluation Date**: 2026-11-09
**Overall Recommendation**: Major Revision
---
### 1. Summary of Appraisal
The report is candid about several artifacts, and the implemented compositing is more careful than the text conveys: QA_PIXEL and ST_QA masking, per-cell window tiers, and a MODIS cross-check. The stratification by core, satellite cities and hinterland is also necessary and well motivated. My concern is the chain from sensor signal to lived exposure to inequality. Landsat surface temperature, from clear-sky mid-morning dry-season scenes, is named "Thermal Stress" and treated as an acute hazard, with no linking assumption, validation or stated limitation. The injustice claim is not tested: the data contain no income measure, and within DKI the anomaly does not follow crowding. Thermal coverage fails for about a third of cells in Jakarta Utara and Barat, where the report places its worst kampungs. Reported ΔLST values do not match the pipeline, and the baseline used is an upland-dominated reference. The slope channel uses a surface model, is zero in most of the delta, and marks tall buildings as terrain hazard, while flood, tide and subsidence are absent. NDVI is greenness, not managed or degraded vegetation. These are repairable, but they require new validation and re-analysis. Major revision is warranted.

### 2. Major Methodological Critiques (P0 / P1)

**Answers to the Seat 3 questions (Section 7.1).**

*Q1. Are single-date or seasonal-composite Landsat-8/9 LST retrievals sufficient for long-term micro-climate vulnerability, given monsoonal cloud masking in Java?* No. A single date is plainly inadequate, and a dry-season composite supports only a statement about relative clear-sky, mid-morning, dry-season surface temperature. In the repository, 74.8% of residential cells use June to September 2023 alone, an El Niño season, so the "2022–2024 Composite" in §3 misdescribes most cells. Cloud masking also removes the wet season by design and leaves coverage holes on the north coast. Long-term claims need a multi-year stack with an annual-cycle model, tier harmonisation, a diurnal source, and validation against air temperature and humid-heat measures.

*Q2. Does a 30 m DEM capture physical hazard exposure in informal settlements, or should hydraulic flood retention depth be prioritised over slope?* A 30 m DEM does not capture it, and flood exposure should take priority over slope in this delta. However, flood depth should not be derived from a global 30 m model, because vertical error is of the same order as typical depths and subsidence dates static elevation quickly. Use RW-level inundation records, InaRISK layers, Sentinel-1 flood frequency, and hydrodynamic modelling on DEMNAS or LiDAR terrain. The phrase "hydraulic flood retention depth" also needs a definition.

*Q3. Does NDVI differentiate managed luxury private greenery from degraded spontaneous vegetation?* No. NDVI measures green leaf signal. It carries no information on tenure, management, access, species or condition, and it saturates over dense canopy and turf. Separating these requires a tree-canopy product, land-use and access data, and socioeconomic testing. I did not complete a golf-course overlay on the repository cells, so this answer rests on sensor physics and the repository's NDVI behaviour, not on a golf-specific test.

- **Point 1 (Conceptual Validity)**: Surface temperature is not heat exposure. Landsat ST_B10 is a directional skin temperature at about 10:00 local time, dominated in kampungs by roofs, and it omits air temperature, humidity, wind, radiant load and indoor conditions. Voogt and Oke (2003), cited by the authors, draws this distinction, and Venter et al. (2021), Chakraborty et al. (2022), Ramsay et al. (2021) and Kuras et al. (2017) show why it matters. The thermal injustice statement in §4.1 is imported and untested. Within DKI, ΔLST correlates with plan area fraction (Spearman 0.54) and density (0.44), but +0.18 with net footprint per capita, opposite to the claimed direction, and -0.08 with modelled space sufficiency. The strong all-cell correlation is the urban-rural gradient. Similarly, the steep-slope displacement mechanism does not suit Jakarta, where flood, tidal inundation and subsidence are the leading physical hazards for poor residents (Abidin et al., 2011; Budiyono et al., 2015; Padawangi and Douglass, 2015). The water-frequency channel was retired without disclosure in the report, and its stated grounds included a 0.024 correlation with living space, which is not a reason to remove a hazard.

- **Point 2 (Data & Metric Integrity)**: Reported ΔLST means are +2.78, +1.42 and -0.38 K for DKI, Kota and Kabupaten; current outputs are +10.90, +9.79 and +5.28 K. The stated 10th-percentile baseline (299.01 K) is not the implemented median baseline (305.45 K), and applying it would widen the gap. The background is dominated by steep rural land: flat background cells have a median of 308.25 K against 304.09 K for steeper ones, so "SUHI intensity" is partly an elevation and land-cover contrast. Rank order does not change, but magnitudes and fixed-bound scaling do. Coverage is the second issue: 894 residential cells (9.6%) have no LST, including 34.2% of Jakarta Utara and 32.9% of Jakarta Barat, and about 1.53 million of 4.51 million residents there. In DKI, failure is 28.9% north of latitude -6.2 and 0.7% south of it. MODIS at 1 km shows no clear temperature difference between failed and retained DKI cells (309.90 and 310.07 K), so this is mainly a coverage problem, but the affected places are those the report features. The cause (cloud or ST_QA limit) needs diagnosing.

- **Point 3 (Artifacts & Confounders)**: GLO-30 is a surface model, and FABDEM is absent from the code. Against the report's 0.0% claim, 184 of 860 DKI cells have non-zero steep share (maximum 9.5%), rising to 48.8% of cells in the top quintile of modelled floors, against 11.6 to 20.4% in lower quintiles. An 8 m rise across 30 m reaches 15 degrees, so building edges are read as terrain. Slope is also a share of cell area, not of inhabited land: the 1,579 cells with at least 5% steep area hold 2.0 million of 40.5 million residents. Thermal tiers are not interchangeable: within mixed 0.1-degree bins, Landsat minus MODIS is about +1.9 K for Tier 2 and -1.2 K for Tier 3, against +0.2 K for Tier 1, comparable to the 2.4 K spread among DKI municipal means, with the caveat that tier is confounded with cloudiness. Landsat-MODIS agreement falls from 0.88 overall to 0.61 within DKI. NDVI is primarily rurality (Spearman with density -0.72), its direction is unresolved between the report and the benchmark (`context`), and it cannot support "lack of tree canopy".

### 3. Minor Technical Concerns (P2)
- Corrugated sheet has low, not high, thermal mass. Metal roof emissivity is poorly represented by the ASTER-based emissivity in the Collection 2 product (Malakar et al., 2018; Laraby and Schott, 2018), and 100 m native TIRS cannot resolve roof materials.
- "Micro-scale" and "micro-climate" overstate a 74 ha mean of 100 m thermal data.
- NDVI cell means include water: 29 residential cells are negative, and the 102 cells below 0.1 have a median water frequency of 0.38. "Vegetation Cover Fraction" on [0, 1] is a misnomer for mean NDVI (minimum -0.241), and the FVC alternative is not implemented.
- Slope is computed by `ee.Terrain.slope` (4-connected neighbours, as documented) after `reproject`, not Horn's method.
- The 15-degree regulatory limit needs a quoted clause. Secondary Indonesian sources I found cite percent-gradient classes (for example 0 to 25% under Permen PU 41/PRT/M/2007). I have not read the regulations themselves.
- Two residential cells at 268.97 and 288.72 K (MODIS near 306 K) suggest residual cloud.
- The report should state who may use hazard labels, at what scale, and how misuse in eviction or relocation decisions is to be avoided.

### 4. Fact-Checking & Comparative Grounding
- *Comparative Megacity Evidence*: Estoque et al. (2017, *Science of the Total Environment*) is the relevant Landsat LST comparison across Bangkok, Jakarta and Manila and links impervious cover to higher and green cover to lower mean LST; it is a different paper from the Estoque reference in the manuscript, whose DOI the audit shows is unrelated. Ramsay et al. (2021) gives indoor heat evidence from tropical informal settlements. Neither establishes that LST ranks residents by heat stress in Jakarta. Comparative flood evidence for the delta setting should come from Jakarta's own hazard record (Budiyono et al., 2015).
- *Empirical Literature Concordance*: The negative LST-NDVI association (all-cell Spearman -0.76) agrees with the surface heat literature. The inferred income gradient does not follow: Chakraborty et al. (2019) tests it with socioeconomic data, whereas this study has none. The "luxury effect" (Hope et al., 2003; Schwarz et al., 2015) and green equity arguments (Wolch et al., 2014) likewise rest on socioeconomic or access data absent here. FABDEM vertical accuracy in built-up areas (about 1 m; Hawker et al., 2022) limits flood-depth use.

### 5. Concrete Actionable Recommendations for Revision
1. Rename ΔLST as a dry-season clear-sky surface temperature anomaly. State the linking assumption and validate against air temperature and a humid-heat index, or drop the "thermal stress" and "acute hazard" language.
2. Report the composite accurately. Add multi-year and annual-cycle estimates, harmonise tiers, and add a diurnal or night-time source if vulnerability remains the claim.
3. Report LST coverage by administrative unit, identify the masking cause, and test sensitivity to the ST_QA limit and to gap handling, especially on the north coast.
4. Reconcile all ΔLST tables with the pipeline, and adopt an elevation-matched lowland background with sensitivity analysis.
5. Test thermal and green inequality with socioeconomic data and a stated disparity measure on residential pixels, or remove the injustice claims.
6. Replace GLO-30 with a bare-earth model at minimum. Compute slope exposure over built footprints or population.
7. Add a validated flood, tidal and subsidence exposure channel, and disclose why the water-frequency channel was retired.
8. Relabel NDVI as greenness, mask water, resolve its direction, and add tree-canopy, land-use and access layers to address managed and private greenery.
9. Quote the planning clause and unit for the slope threshold, and correct the slope algorithm description.
10. State intended users, scale and misuse limits for the hazard layers, with community or BPBD input to validation.
