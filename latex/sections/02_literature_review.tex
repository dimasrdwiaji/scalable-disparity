\section{Literature Review: Indicator and Dataset Vetting}

Evaluating which open global indicators can detect intra-urban deprivation requires systematic vetting rather than assembling every available layer. This section reviews candidate physical, socioeconomic, and spatial-analysis proxies to answer \textbf{RQ1}, establishing which variables distort block-level living conditions and which physical indicators genuinely measure what they claim to measure across Indonesian urban settings.

\subsection{Area-Level Physical Deprivation and Gradual Consolidation}
International deprivation frameworks such as the Integrated Deprived Area Mapping System (IDEAMAPS; \citealt{Thomson2020}), the Domains of Deprivation framework \citep{Abascal2022a}, and the English Indices of Deprivation \citep{MHCLG2019} separate neighborhood built conditions and micro-environmental quality from household income and demographic characteristics. That separation matters when monitoring physical infrastructure disparities, because asset-wealth surfaces predicted from survey targets and connectivity data, most prominently Meta's Relative Wealth Index \citep{Chi2022}, estimate household durable ownership rather than built housing adequacy \citep{Lilford2017}. Asset-based proxies therefore fall outside the physical domain examined here.

Early remote sensing studies often reduced informal settlement mapping to a binary split between ``slum'' and ``formal'' areas \citep{Kuffer2016}. As \citet{Dovey2020} show, informal settlements rarely exist in a static binary state; households upgrade structures incrementally over time, replacing timber walls with brick and concrete while narrow alleys and lack of open setback remain unchanged. Greater Jakarta illustrates this gradual consolidation directly. Inner-city \textit{kampungs} adjacent to central districts such as Menteng and Cikini frequently contain multi-story brick dwellings beside narrow footpaths and formal apartment blocks \citep{Hidayati2020, Mardhotillah2018}, whereas suburban settlements in Tangerang range from semi-permanent riverside pockets near industrial corridors to consolidated neighborhoods bordering gated estates \citep{Wardhani2016}. Recognizing that graded reality, official municipal inventories in DKI Jakarta (Governor Regulation No.~90/2018) classify neighborhoods across four severity categories rather than a single cutoff, supporting continuous or multi-domain indicators over binary labels \citep{Abascal2022b}.

\subsection{Disqualified Proxies: Density Confounding, Coarse Sensors, and Why Accessibility Fails}
Many open global layers reflect how large or busy a city is rather than inequality within it. In practice, off-the-shelf indicators mislead neighborhood mapping in three common ways. First, overall density is not the same thing as deprivation: luxury high-rise districts and crowded low-income \textit{kampungs} can both show high building density, so density alone cannot separate them \citep{Taubenbock2018}. Second, derived ratios become circular when both the numerator and denominator depend on the same underlying map: if a population grid already allocates people onto cells based on mapped buildings, dividing building area back by that population estimate repeats the building layer rather than measuring actual household crowding. Third, an indicator can flip meaning between dense city centers and the rural fringe \citep{Chakraborty2019a, Stewart2012}: a poor village surrounded by rice paddies or riverside trees looks cool and green in satellite images, whereas a wealthy commercial center looks hot and paved. Comparing neighborhoods only against their urban peers avoids this misleading flip. Across these checks, four physical proxy groups prove unsuitable for neighborhood deprivation mapping, alongside spatial accessibility metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Building-allocated per-capita living space:} Population grids such as GHSL-POP \citep{Pesaresi2024, Schiavina2023} and WorldPop \citep{Stevens2015} disaggregate census counts to cells primarily using built-up footprint and volume. Dividing residential building volume back by that modeled population to estimate living space per person \citep{UNHabitat2020} creates a circular loop where both numerator and denominator come from the same building layer. The resulting ratio reflects the population model's allocation weights rather than true household crowding, yet intra-urban audits of this circularity in living-condition indices remain sparse.
    \item \textbf{Nighttime light radiance (VIIRS):} Nighttime lights track regional economic output well \citep{Henderson2012, Mellander2015}, yet coarse pixel footprints, light spillover across neighboring areas, and bright city-center saturation obscure darker informal pockets embedded inside bright cities and bias comparisons across secondary towns \citep{Gibson2021, Zhao2019}.
    \item \textbf{Coarse atmospheric, thermal, and elevation layers:} Satellite-derived fine particulate ($\text{PM}_{2.5}$) estimates operate near 1--5~km resolution suited to regional health studies rather than street-block disparity \citep{vanDonkelaar2021}. Similarly, 1~km MODIS thermal imagery averages across neighboring city blocks \citep{Voogt2003}, and unfiltered global elevation models read tall formal buildings as steep terrain slope \citep{Hawker2022}.
    \item \textbf{Unmasked building coverage:} Raw building-footprint coverage mixes residential crowding together with large industrial warehouses, port terminals, and shopping malls \citep{Chamberlain2024, Jochem2021, Sirko2021}.
\end{itemize}

Standard pedestrian accessibility metrics---including cumulative 15-minute opportunity counts \citep{Handy1997, Hosford2022, Moreno2021, Weiss2018} and Two-Step Floating Catchment Area (2SFCA) scores \citep{Geurs2004, Luo2003}---are likewise excluded, even with street-network routing \citep{Boeing2017} and Overture Maps points of interest \citep{Overture2023, Overture2024}. Two barriers prevent open proximity scores from measuring true service access. First, crowdsourced facility attributes such as operator type, fees, bed counts, and sidewalk quality thin out rapidly beyond core city centers \citep{BarringtonLeigh2017, Herfort2023}.

Second, physical closeness reverses socioeconomic status in Indonesian cities. Central Jakarta \textit{kampungs} sit beside clinics, schools, markets, and transit lines, making residents ``captive pedestrians'' with dense nearby destinations yet constrained daily mobility \citep{Hidayati2020}, whereas wealthy gated estates often sit farther from commercial streets \citep{Firman2004}. Because open pins omit price and eligibility, proximity scores count private hospitals and schools that low-income households cannot afford, inverting deprivation rankings.

\subsection{Vetting Built Form, the \texorpdfstring{50~m$^2$}{50 m2} Threshold, and Roof-Material Texture}
Once factories, warehouses, ports, offices, and commercial halls mapped in OpenStreetMap (OSM) are removed and GHSL residential share is tracked conditionally to separate industrial encroachment \citep{Pesaresi2024}, building geometry provides clear physical signals inside urban cores \citep{Abascal2022b, Jochem2021b, Kuffer2016}. Two geometric indicators stand out from open building footprints: the share of \textbf{small-footprint residential buildings} and nearest-neighbor building spacing \citep{Jochem2021, Taubenbock2018}. Prior remote-sensing studies in Indonesian cities ground the small-footprint boundary around 50--60~m$^2$. \citet{Najmi2022} showed that filtering residential structures below 60~m$^2$ aligns footprint classifications closely with official Indonesian municipal slum maps, supporting local classifications in Bandung \citep{Leonita2018} and building-size criteria reviewed by \citet{Kuffer2016}. Under Indonesian residential planning standards (SNI 03-1733-2004; \citealt{SNI2004}), basic planned formal housing units anchor around dedicated multi-room starter footprints, whereas unbuffered self-built \textit{kampung} houses pack densely below 50~m$^2$ with tight gaps between roofs. Checking $<50\text{ m}^2$ alongside $<60\text{ m}^2$ ties footprint size directly to local housing form without relying on population ratios.

Contrasting roof materials---corrugated metal, asbestos, and weathered patching versus uniform clay tiles or concrete---also signal housing deprivation \citep{Kuffer2016, Kuffer2016b, Wurm2017}. While individual \textit{kampung} roofs are smaller than a 10~m Sentinel-2 pixel, Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) statistics measure how sharply brightness changes between neighboring pixels across multi-pixel windows, capturing patch-level roof heterogeneity \citep{deMattos2021, Wurm2017b} without the radar layover distortions seen in dense blocks \citep{Wurm2017}. Because residential-masked Sentinel-2 GLCM remains sparsely tested against raw building density, it is retained strictly as a candidate indicator pending empirical screening.

\subsection{Micro-Environmental Baselines, Inequality Metrics, and Boundary Models}
Raw vegetation and temperature readings mislead when dense urban blocks are compared against distant forests or rice paddies \citep{Chakraborty2019a, Stewart2012}. Referencing Sentinel-2 Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Landsat land surface temperature ($\Delta\text{LST}$) against built-up urban baselines isolates within-city environmental inequity, showing where neighborhoods have less canopy and higher surface heat than comparable urban areas \citep{Chakraborty2019b, Hsu2021}. Neighborhood heat mapping therefore requires multi-year clear-sky 100~m Landsat 8/9 thermal composites rather than 1~km MODIS products that blur block-scale hotspots \citep{Voogt2003}; flood-frequency and wetness layers remain outside our scope.

Unweighted averages allow generous tree canopy to mask severe housing crowding, so each domain must be validated alone before testing penalty-adjusted aggregation that penalizes imbalance across domains \citep{Mazziotta2016, OECD2008}. On an urban-masked H3 grid, hexagonal Gini coefficients and Lorenz curves adapted from \texttt{greenR} \citep{Mahajan2024} and spatial Gini analysis \citep{Rey2013} summarize how unevenly living conditions vary across space. Since inequality metrics describe spatial spread rather than who lives in poor housing, they require known-group validation against official records.

To locate abrupt formal--informal transitions without label supervision, we benchmark an unsupervised Graph Attention Autoencoder (GAT-AE; \citealt{Salehi2020, Velickovic2018})---whose edge attention highlights abrupt jumps between neighboring hexagons \citep{Ding2019}---directly against Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA; \citealt{Anselin1995}) and linear spatial gradients under spatially blocked cross-validation \citep{Roberts2017}.

\subsection{Conceptual Synthesis: Built Form vs. Micro-Environment Typology}
Green canopy and housing quality do not always move together: a leafy peri-urban \textit{kampung} still lacks formal housing layout, while a paved downtown business block looks barren and hot without being a slum.
