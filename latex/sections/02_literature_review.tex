\section{Literature Review: Indicator and Dataset Vetting}

Evaluating which open global indicators can detect intra-urban deprivation requires systematic vetting rather than assembling every available layer. This section reviews candidate physical, socioeconomic, and spatial-analysis proxies to answer \textbf{RQ1}, establishing which variables distort block-level living conditions and which physical indicators genuinely measure what they claim to measure across Indonesian urban settings.

\subsection{Area-Level Physical Deprivation and Gradual Consolidation}
International deprivation frameworks such as the Integrated Deprived Area Mapping System (IDEAMAPS; \citealt{Thomson2020}), the Domains of Deprivation framework \citep{Abascal2022a}, and the English Indices of Deprivation \citep{MHCLG2019} separate neighborhood built conditions and micro-environmental quality from household income and demographic characteristics. That separation matters when monitoring physical infrastructure disparities, because asset-wealth surfaces predicted from survey targets and connectivity data, most prominently Meta's Relative Wealth Index \citep{Chi2022}, estimate household durable ownership rather than built housing adequacy \citep{Lilford2017}. Asset-based proxies therefore fall outside the physical domain examined here.

Early remote sensing studies often reduced informal settlement mapping to a binary split between ``slum'' and ``formal'' areas \citep{Kuffer2016}. As \citet{Dovey2020} show, informal settlements rarely exist in a static binary state; households upgrade structures incrementally over time, replacing timber walls with brick and concrete while narrow alleys and lack of open setback remain unchanged. Greater Jakarta illustrates this gradual consolidation directly. Inner-city \textit{kampungs} adjacent to central districts such as Menteng and Cikini frequently contain multi-story brick dwellings beside narrow footpaths and formal apartment blocks \citep{Hidayati2020, Mardhotillah2018}, whereas suburban settlements in Tangerang range from semi-permanent riverside pockets near industrial corridors to consolidated neighborhoods bordering gated estates \citep{Wardhani2016}. Recognizing that graded reality, official municipal inventories in DKI Jakarta (Governor Regulation No.~90/2018) classify neighborhoods across four severity categories rather than a single cutoff, supporting continuous or multi-domain indicators over binary labels \citep{Abascal2022b}.

\subsection{Disqualified Proxies: Density Confounding, Coarse Sensors, and Why Accessibility Fails}
Many open global layers reflect how large or busy a city is rather than inequality within it. In practice, off-the-shelf indicators mislead neighborhood mapping in three common ways. First, overall density is not the same thing as deprivation: luxury high-rise districts and crowded low-income \textit{kampungs} can both show high building density, so density alone cannot separate them \citep{Taubenbock2018}. Second, derived ratios become circular when both the numerator and denominator depend on the same underlying map: if a population grid already allocates people onto cells based on mapped buildings, dividing building area back by that population estimate repeats the building layer rather than measuring actual household crowding. Third, an indicator can flip meaning between dense city centers and the rural fringe \citep{Chakraborty2019a, Stewart2012}: a poor village surrounded by rice paddies or riverside trees looks cool and green in satellite images, whereas a wealthy commercial center looks hot and paved. Comparing neighborhoods only against their urban peers avoids this misleading flip. Across these checks, four physical proxy groups prove unsuitable for neighborhood deprivation mapping, alongside spatial accessibility metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Building-allocated per-capita living space:} Global population grids such as GHSL-POP \citep{Pesaresi2024, Schiavina2023} and WorldPop \citep{Stevens2015} spread census counts down to grid cells mainly by looking at where building volume sits. Dividing residential building volume back by that estimated population to approximate minimum living space per person \citep{UNHabitat2020}, both numbers draw from the same building data. Avoiding this circular loop is critical, because the resulting score mostly reflects the population model's allocation rule rather than real overcrowding inside homes. While population-disaggregation methods are widely published, empirical audits testing this specific intra-urban loop inside living-condition indices remain sparse.
    \item \textbf{Nighttime light radiance (VIIRS):} Nighttime lights track regional economic output well \citep{Henderson2012, Mellander2015}, yet coarse pixel footprints, light spillover across neighboring areas, and bright city-center saturation obscure darker informal pockets embedded inside bright cities and bias comparisons across secondary towns \citep{Gibson2021, Zhao2019}.
    \item \textbf{Coarse atmospheric, thermal, and elevation layers:} Satellite-derived fine particulate ($\text{PM}_{2.5}$) estimates operate near 1--5~km resolution suited to regional health studies rather than street-block disparity \citep{vanDonkelaar2021}. Similarly, 1~km MODIS thermal imagery averages across neighboring city blocks \citep{Voogt2003}, and unfiltered global elevation models read tall formal buildings as steep terrain slope \citep{Hawker2022}.
    \item \textbf{Unmasked building coverage:} Raw building-footprint coverage mixes residential crowding together with large industrial warehouses, port terminals, and shopping malls \citep{Chamberlain2024, Jochem2021, Sirko2021}.
\end{itemize}

Standard pedestrian accessibility measures, including cumulative 15-minute opportunity counts \citep{Handy1997, Hosford2022, Moreno2021, Weiss2018} and competition-adjusted gravity or Two-Step Floating Catchment Area (2SFCA) scores \citep{Geurs2004, Luo2003}, must also be excluded from the physical deprivation model, even when paired with street-network routing \citep{Boeing2017} and standardized Overture Maps categories \citep{Overture2023, Overture2024}. Two practical barriers explain this decision. First, volunteer-mapped attributes such as public versus private operators, fees, bed counts, and sidewalk presence become scarce outside major city centers \citep{BarringtonLeigh2017, Herfort2023}. Second, spatial proximity does not measure economic access in Indonesian cities. In Jakarta, low-income \textit{kampungs} sit inside central mixed-use areas surrounded by clinics, schools, markets, and transit lines, making residents ``captive pedestrians'' with many nearby destinations yet constrained daily mobility \citep{Hidayati2020}, while wealthier gated estates often sit farther away from busy commercial streets \citep{Firman2004}. Whether low-income households have the financial means or eligibility to use private hospitals or schools nearby cannot be observed from open point-of-interest pins. Without price, quality, and eligibility data, neither cumulative counts nor gravity/2SFCA models separate physical closeness from real ability to use a service, reversing deprivation rankings across central informal settlements.

\subsection{Vetting Built Form, the \texorpdfstring{50~m$^2$}{50 m2} Threshold, and Roof-Material Texture}
Once factories, warehouses, ports, offices, and commercial halls mapped in OpenStreetMap (OSM) are removed and GHSL residential share is tracked conditionally to separate industrial encroachment \citep{Pesaresi2024}, building geometry provides clear physical signals inside urban cores \citep{Abascal2022b, Jochem2021b, Kuffer2016}. Two geometric indicators stand out from open building footprints: the share of \textbf{small-footprint residential buildings} and nearest-neighbor building spacing \citep{Jochem2021, Taubenbock2018}. Prior remote-sensing studies in Indonesian cities ground the small-footprint boundary around 50--60~m$^2$. \citet{Najmi2022} showed that filtering residential structures below 60~m$^2$ aligns footprint classifications closely with official Indonesian municipal slum maps, supporting local classifications in Bandung \citep{Leonita2018} and building-size criteria reviewed by \citet{Kuffer2016}. Under Indonesian residential planning standards (SNI 03-1733-2004; \citealt{SNI2004}), basic planned formal housing units anchor around dedicated multi-room starter footprints, whereas unbuffered self-built \textit{kampung} houses pack densely below 50~m$^2$ with tight gaps between roofs. Checking $<50\text{ m}^2$ alongside $<60\text{ m}^2$ ties footprint size directly to local housing form without relying on population ratios.

Beyond building size and spacing, contrasting roof materials, such as corrugated metal sheets, asbestos panels, weathered plastic, and patched tiles compared with uniform clay tile or concrete slabs, are an established sign of housing quality deprivation \citep{Kuffer2016, Kuffer2016b, Wurm2017}. A key open-data question is whether free 10~m Sentinel-2 optical imagery can detect that contrast reliably. Because individual small roofs are often smaller than a 10~m pixel, single-roof material classification is impossible. However, Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) texture features computed across multi-pixel neighborhood windows capture broader surface variation across roof-and-alley patches in Sentinel-2 imagery \citep{deMattos2021, Wurm2017b}, while avoiding the side-looking geometric distortions that make C-band radar hard to interpret in tight urban blocks \citep{Wurm2017}. Since GLCM restricted to residential footprints has seen limited testing against urban density, residential-masked 10~m Sentinel-2 GLCM is retained as a candidate indicator rather than a confirmed core channel.

\subsection{Micro-Environmental Baselines, Inequality Metrics, and Boundary Models}
For green and thermal conditions, raw vegetation and temperature readings fail when dense city neighborhoods are compared against distant mountain forests or wet rice paddies \citep{Chakraborty2019a, Stewart2012}. Normalizing Sentinel-2 Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Landsat land surface temperature ($\Delta\text{LST}$) against each city's own urban built area isolates within-city environmental injustice. in turn showing which neighborhoods face thinner tree cover and hotter surfaces than other similarly-built areas \citep{Chakraborty2019b, Hsu2021}. Sensor scale also shapes dataset selection: neighborhood heat mapping requires multi-year clear-sky composites from 100~m Landsat 8/9 thermal bands rather than 1~km MODIS fallbacks that blur individual blocks \citep{Voogt2003}. Flood-frequency and terrain wetness layers stay out of scope.

When combining built-form and micro-environmental scores, simple unweighted averages let generous tree canopy hide severe residential crowding, so domains are validated separately first and combined using penalty-adjusted aggregation that penalizes extreme imbalance \citep{Mazziotta2016, OECD2008}. Over an urban-masked H3 hexagonal grid, citywide inequality summaries adapted from \texttt{greenR} \citep{Mahajan2024} and spatial Gini analysis \citep{Rey2013}, hexagonal Gini coefficients and Lorenz curves, quantify how unevenly physical quality is distributed across the city. Because inequality curves only describe spread across space without telling us who experiences deprivation, they must be paired with known-group validation against official records. Finally, to test whether deep graph models locate formal--informal boundaries more sharply than classical spatial statistics, an unsupervised Graph Attention Autoencoder (GAT-AE; \citealt{Salehi2020, Velickovic2018}) tracing which indicators drive abrupt jumps between neighboring cells \citep{Ding2019} must be benchmarked directly against Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA; \citealt{Anselin1995}) and spatial gradients under spatially blocked cross-validation \citep{Roberts2017}, as direct comparisons on informal settlement boundaries remain scarce.

\subsection{Conceptual Synthesis: Built Form vs. Micro-Environment Typology}
Replacing earlier wealth-versus-living-standard comparisons with strictly physical, vetted dimensions yields a clear two-axis typology across urban space (Figure~\ref{fig:quadrant_typology}). The vertical axis tracks residential built form ($y+$: formal residential fabric with larger footprints, wider spacing, and uniform roofs; $y-$: informal residential fabric dominated by small-footprint residential buildings, tight packing, and mixed roofing). The horizontal axis tracks intra-urban micro-environmental buffering ($x+$: vegetated canopy and neutral or cooler urban surface temperatures; $x-$: canopy deficit and elevated intra-urban surface heat). Keeping these two dimensions separate explains why single-indicator maps misclassify neighborhoods---for instance, mistaking greener peri-urban \textit{kampungs} (Quadrant~III) for adequate housing or confusing hot, paved formal commercial districts (Quadrant~II) with slums---and motivates checking both domains separately before boundary testing.
