\section{Introduction}

Physical living conditions in growing cities rarely change smoothly across administrative districts. High-density informal settlements, often called \textit{kampungs} in Indonesia, frequently border formal housing estates and commercial centers directly \citep{Kuffer2016, Taubenbock2018}. Greater Jakarta (Jabodetabek) illustrates this proximity clearly. Gated residential developments and apartment blocks stand immediately adjacent to low-income \textit{kampungs}, divided only by perimeter walls or arterial streets in central neighborhoods such as Menteng and Cikini as well as in suburban Tangerang \citep{Hidayati2020, Mardhotillah2018, Wardhani2016}.

\subsection{Detecting Intra-Urban Disparity with Open Data}
Locating these neighborhood-scale differences supports slum upgrading, infrastructure planning, and heat mitigation \citep{Abascal2022a, Chakraborty2019b, Hsu2021, Leonita2018}. When conditions are reported only as municipal or district averages, contrasting neighborhoods cancel each other out, hiding deprived pockets inside statistically affluent districts \citep{Openshaw1984, Reardon2004}. Spatial indicators mapped onto uniform hexagonal grids, paired with urban inequality summaries such as Gini or Palma ratios \citep{Mahajan2024, Rey2013}, show where local deficit occurs and how unevenly they are distributed across the observed area.

Across much of the Global South, however, detailed municipal cadastres, sub-district housing registries, and commercial very-high-resolution imagery are incomplete, outdated, or restricted \citep{Herfort2023, Thomson2020}. Monitoring tools therefore have to rely on globally open geospatial datasets. Even open data require scrutiny before entering a model. Coarse atmospheric or thermal products blur street-level contrasts \citep{vanDonkelaar2021, Voogt2003}, population grids and nighttime lights tend to follow overall built density rather than local housing quality \citep{Gibson2021, Pesaresi2024}, and raw amenity counts can invert when central \textit{kampungs} sit surrounded by busy commercial streets while wealthier gated estates sit farther away \citep{Hidayati2020}.

\subsection{Indicator Vetting and Analytical Scope}
Indicator selection cannot rely on data availability alone because poor or mismatched inputs bias the inequality result and clustering models. This study treats literature-based \textbf{indicator vetting} as a core methodological step. Candidate physical, environmental, and accessibility datasets are evaluated against four practical screening checks before modeling: (1) whether pixel or polygon size captures individual city blocks, (2) whether derived ratios avoid circular calculations against building-allocated population grids or overall urban density, (3) whether farmland or non-residential land uses flip the direction of the signal, and (4) whether crowdsourced tags leave out poorer or secondary-city neighborhoods \citep{BarringtonLeigh2017, Herfort2023, Schiavina2023}.

Section~2 conducts this full vetting review to establish which datasets warrant inclusion and what cutoffs or spatial masks they require, narrowing the scope to area-level built form and micro-environmental quality rather than consumer asset models such as Meta's Relative Wealth Index \citep{Chi2022}, household crowding ratios, or service-proximity accessibility indices (which Section~2 excludes due to missing affordability data and proximity inversion in central \textit{kampungs}). On an Uber H3 Resolution-8 grid ($\approx 531\text{ m}$ edge length) covering Mainland DKI Jakarta, Karawang, and an external hold-out city, the vetted feature pool covers two physical domains: residential built form (small-footprint residential buildings, nearest-neighbor building spacing, residential built-up share, and candidate Sentinel-2 roof-texture metrics) and intra-urban green-thermal conditions (Sentinel-2 canopy greenness and 100~m Landsat thermal anomalies referenced against each city's urban extent). Each retained variable is first examined on its own to see how well it identifies informal areas and what side of disparity it explains, before combining validated domains so strength in one area cannot mask severe deficits in another \citep{Mazziotta2016, OECD2008}. We also compute urban-masked hexagonal inequality metrics and test whether an unsupervised Spatial Graph Attention Autoencoder (GAT-AE; \citealt{Salehi2020, Velickovic2018}) with a two-dimensional bottleneck ($d=2$) outperforms Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA; \citealt{Anselin1995}) and linear spatial gradients under spatially blocked cross-validation \citep{Roberts2017}.

\subsection{Research Questions}
Three research questions structure the investigation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1}: Which open physical and environmental indicators, and their associated global datasets, are suitable for identifying intra-urban deprivation once vetted for spatial resolution, density scaling, non-residential mixing, and proximity bias?
    \item \textbf{RQ2}: How does each vetted indicator perform on its own, and when combined across physical domains without allowing surpluses to mask deficits, in identifying informal settlements, explaining different sides of neighborhood disparity, and describing citywide inequality?
    \item \textbf{RQ3}: Does an unsupervised Spatial GAT-AE built on these vetted indicators isolate formal--informal neighborhood boundaries more accurately than classical spatial statistics under spatially blocked cross-validation, and how consistently does the method transfer across severity grades and an external city?
\end{enumerate}

Section~2 answers RQ1 through the indicator-vetting literature review; Section~3 describes the study areas and open-data preparation; Section~4 details per-indicator validation, inequality metrics, and the GAT-AE benchmark; and Sections~5--7 present empirical results, limitations, and conclusions.
