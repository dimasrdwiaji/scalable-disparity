# 2. Literature Review: Indicator and Dataset Vetting

Evaluating which open global indicators can detect intra-urban deprivation requires systematic vetting rather than assembling every available layer. This section reviews candidate physical, socioeconomic, and spatial-analysis proxies to answer **RQ1**, establishing which variables distort block-level living conditions and which physical indicators genuinely measure what they claim to measure across Indonesian urban settings.

## 2.1 Area-Level Physical Deprivation and Gradual Consolidation
International deprivation frameworks such as the Integrated Deprived Area Mapping System (IDEAMAPS; [Thomson et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/socsci9050080)), the Domains of Deprivation framework ([Abascal et al., 2022a](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2022.101770)), and the English Indices of Deprivation ([MHCLG, 2019](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/english-indices-of-deprivation-2019)) separate neighborhood built conditions and micro-environmental quality from household income and demographic characteristics. That separation matters when monitoring physical infrastructure disparities, because asset-wealth surfaces predicted from survey targets and connectivity data—most prominently Meta's Relative Wealth Index ([Chi et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2113658119))—estimate household durable ownership rather than built housing adequacy ([Lilford et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)31848-7)). Asset-based proxies therefore fall outside the physical domain examined here.

Early remote sensing studies often reduced informal settlement mapping to a binary split between "slum" and "formal" areas ([Kuffer et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs8060455)). As [Dovey et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.habitatint.2020.102324) show, informal settlements rarely exist in a static binary state; households upgrade structures incrementally over time, replacing timber walls with brick and concrete while narrow alleys and lack of open setback remain unchanged. Greater Jakarta illustrates this gradual consolidation directly. Inner-city *kampungs* adjacent to central districts such as Menteng and Cikini frequently contain multi-story brick dwellings beside narrow footpaths and formal apartment blocks ([Hidayati et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/17549175.2020.1814391); [Mardhotillah & Gamal, 2018](https://doi.org/10.14716/ijtech.v9i7.2519)), whereas suburban settlements in Tangerang range from semi-permanent riverside pockets near industrial corridors to consolidated neighborhoods bordering gated estates ([Wardhani, 2016](https://doi.org/10.18196/jgp.2016.0025)). Recognizing that graded reality, official municipal inventories in DKI Jakarta (Governor Regulation No. 90/2018) classify neighborhoods across four severity categories rather than a single cutoff, supporting continuous or multi-domain indicators over binary labels ([Abascal et al., 2022b](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2022.101820)).

## 2.2 Disqualified Proxies: Density Confounding, Coarse Sensors, and Why Accessibility Fails
Many open global layers reflect how large or busy a city is rather than inequality within it. In practice, off-the-shelf indicators mislead neighborhood mapping in three common ways. First, overall density is not the same thing as deprivation: luxury high-rise districts and crowded low-income *kampungs* can both show high building density, so density alone cannot separate them ([Taubenböck et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apgeog.2018.02.002)). Second, derived ratios become circular when both the numerator and denominator depend on the same underlying map: if a population grid already allocates people onto cells based on mapped buildings, dividing building area back by that population estimate repeats the building layer rather than measuring actual household crowding. Third, an indicator can flip meaning between dense city centers and the rural fringe ([Chakraborty & Lee, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jag.2018.09.015); [Stewart & Oke, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1175/BAMS-D-11-00019.1)): a poor village surrounded by rice paddies or riverside trees looks cool and green in satellite images, whereas a wealthy commercial center looks hot and paved. Comparing neighborhoods only against their urban peers avoids this misleading flip. Across these checks, four physical proxy groups prove unsuitable for neighborhood deprivation mapping, alongside spatial accessibility metrics:

- **Building-allocated per-capita living space:** Population grids such as GHSL-POP ([Pesaresi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/17538947.2024.2390454); [Schiavina et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.2760/098587)) and WorldPop ([Stevens et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0107042)) disaggregate census counts to cells primarily using built-up footprint and volume. Dividing residential building volume back by that modeled population to estimate living space per person ([UN-Habitat, 2020](https://unhabitat.org/)) creates a circular loop where both numerator and denominator come from the same building layer. The resulting ratio reflects the population model's allocation weights rather than true household crowding, yet intra-urban audits of this circularity in living-condition indices remain sparse.
- **Nighttime light radiance (VIIRS):** Nighttime lights track regional economic output well ([Henderson et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.102.2.994); [Mellander et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0139779)), yet coarse pixel footprints, light spillover across neighboring areas, and bright city-center saturation obscure darker informal pockets embedded inside bright cities and bias comparisons across secondary towns ([Gibson et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2020.102602); [Zhao et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1109/TGRS.2019.2949797)).
- **Coarse atmospheric, thermal, and elevation layers:** Satellite-derived fine particulate ($\text{PM}_{2.5}$) estimates operate near `1–5 km` resolution suited to regional health studies rather than street-block disparity ([van Donkelaar et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.1c05309)). Similarly, `1 km` MODIS thermal imagery averages across neighboring city blocks ([Voogt & Oke, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0034-4257(03)00079-8)), and unfiltered global elevation models read tall formal buildings as steep terrain slope ([Hawker et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/ac4d4f)).
- **Unmasked building coverage:** Raw building-footprint coverage mixes residential crowding together with large industrial warehouses, port terminals, and shopping malls ([Chamberlain et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2024.102104); [Jochem et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/2399808320921208); [Sirko et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12283)).

Standard pedestrian accessibility metrics—including cumulative 15-minute opportunity counts ([Handy & Niemeier, 1997](https://doi.org/10.1068/a291175); [Hosford et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.32866/001c.32454); [Moreno et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/smartcities4010006); [Weiss et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25181)) and Two-Step Floating Catchment Area (2SFCA) scores ([Geurs & van Wee, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtrangeo.2003.10.005); [Luo & Wang, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1068/b29120))—are likewise excluded, even with street-network routing ([Boeing, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2017.05.004)) and Overture Maps points of interest ([Overture Maps Foundation, 2023](https://overturemaps.org), [2024](https://docs.overturemaps.org/gers/)). Two barriers prevent open proximity scores from measuring true service access. First, crowdsourced facility attributes such as operator type, fees, bed counts, and sidewalk quality thin out rapidly beyond core city centers ([Barrington-Leigh & Millard-Ball, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0180698); [Herfort et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-39698-6)).

Second, physical closeness reverses socioeconomic status in Indonesian cities. Central Jakarta *kampungs* sit beside clinics, schools, markets, and transit lines, making residents "captive pedestrians" with dense nearby destinations yet constrained daily mobility ([Hidayati et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/17549175.2020.1814391)), whereas wealthy gated estates often sit farther from commercial streets ([Firman, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0197-3975(03)00037-7)). Because open pins omit price and eligibility, proximity scores count private hospitals and schools that low-income households cannot afford, inverting deprivation rankings.

## 2.3 Vetting Built Form, the 50 m² Threshold, and Roof-Material Texture
Once factories, warehouses, ports, offices, and commercial halls mapped in OpenStreetMap (OSM) are removed and GHSL residential share is tracked conditionally to separate industrial encroachment ([Pesaresi et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/17538947.2024.2390454)), building geometry provides clear physical signals inside urban cores ([Abascal et al., 2022b](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compenvurbsys.2022.101820); [Jochem & Tatem, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0247535); [Kuffer et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs8060455)). Two geometric indicators stand out from open building footprints: the share of **small-footprint residential buildings** and nearest-neighbor building spacing ([Jochem et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/2399808320921208); [Taubenböck et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apgeog.2018.02.002)). Prior remote-sensing studies in Indonesian cities ground the small-footprint boundary around `50–60 m²`. [Najmi et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijgi11120631) showed that filtering residential structures below `60 m²` aligns footprint classifications closely with official Indonesian municipal slum maps, supporting local classifications in Bandung ([Leonita et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs10101522)) and building-size criteria reviewed by [Kuffer et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs8060455). Under Indonesian residential planning standards (SNI 03-1733-2004), basic planned formal housing units anchor around dedicated multi-room starter footprints, whereas unbuffered self-built *kampung* houses pack densely below `50 m²` with tight gaps between roofs. Checking `< 50 m²` alongside `< 60 m²` ties footprint size directly to local housing form without relying on population ratios.

Contrasting roof materials—corrugated metal, asbestos, and weathered patching versus uniform clay tiles or concrete—also signal housing deprivation ([Kuffer et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs8060455); [Kuffer et al., 2016b](https://doi.org/10.1109/JSTARS.2016.2538563); [Wurm et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rse.2017.03.030)). While individual *kampung* roofs are smaller than a `10 m` Sentinel-2 pixel, Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) statistics measure how sharply brightness changes between neighboring pixels across multi-pixel windows, capturing patch-level roof heterogeneity ([de Mattos et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2106.11395); [Wurm et al., 2017b](https://doi.org/10.1109/JURSE.2017.7924586)) without the radar layover distortions seen in dense blocks ([Wurm et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rse.2017.03.030)). Because residential-masked Sentinel-2 GLCM remains sparsely tested against raw building density, it is retained strictly as a candidate indicator pending empirical screening.

## 2.4 Micro-Environmental Baselines, Inequality Metrics, and Boundary Models
Raw vegetation and temperature readings mislead when dense urban blocks are compared against distant forests or rice paddies ([Chakraborty & Lee, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jag.2018.09.015); [Stewart & Oke, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1175/BAMS-D-11-00019.1)). Referencing Sentinel-2 Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (`NDVI`) and Landsat land surface temperature (`ΔLST`) against built-up urban baselines isolates within-city environmental inequity, showing where neighborhoods have less canopy and higher surface heat than comparable urban areas ([Chakraborty et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/ab3b99); [Hsu et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-22799-5)). Neighborhood heat mapping therefore requires multi-year clear-sky `100 m` Landsat 8/9 thermal composites rather than `1 km` MODIS products that blur block-scale hotspots ([Voogt & Oke, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0034-4257(03)00079-8)); flood-frequency and wetness layers remain outside our scope.

Unweighted averages allow generous tree canopy to mask severe housing crowding, so each domain must be validated alone before testing penalty-adjusted aggregation that penalizes imbalance across domains ([Mazziotta & Pareto, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11205-015-0998-2); [OECD/EC-JRC, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1787/9789264043466-en)). On an urban-masked H3 grid, hexagonal Gini coefficients and Lorenz curves adapted from `greenR` ([Mahajan, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecolind.2024.112108)) and spatial Gini analysis ([Rey & Smith, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12076-012-0086-z)) summarize how unevenly living conditions vary across space. Since inequality metrics describe spatial spread rather than who lives in poor housing, they require known-group validation against official records.

To locate abrupt formal–informal transitions without label supervision, we benchmark an unsupervised Graph Attention Autoencoder (`GAT-AE`; [Salehi & Davulcu, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICTAI50040.2020.00154); [Veličković et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1710.10903))—whose edge attention highlights abrupt jumps between neighboring hexagons ([Ding et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1137/1.9781611975673.67))—directly against Local Indicators of Spatial Association (`LISA`; [Anselin, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1538-4632.1995.tb00338.x)) and linear spatial gradients under spatially blocked cross-validation ([Roberts et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.02881)).

## 2.5 Conceptual Synthesis: Built Form vs. Micro-Environment Typology
Replacing earlier wealth-versus-living-standard comparisons with strictly physical, vetted dimensions yields a clear two-axis typology across urban space:

```
                  y+: Formal Residential Fabric
          (Larger Footprints, Wider Spacing, Uniform Roofs)
                                  ▲
                                  │
             Quadrant II          │          Quadrant I
         (Formal Hardscape        │   (Formal Buffered Enclaves:
          Districts: Paved        │    Planned Estates, Canopy,
          Blocks + Heat Stress)   │    Cooler Micro-Climate)
                                  │
x-: Stressed Environment ─────────┼─────────► x+: Buffered Environment
(Canopy Deficit, High ΔLST)       │       (Higher NDVI, Lower ΔLST)
                                  │
             Quadrant IV          │         Quadrant III
         (Compound Deprivation:   │   (Vegetated / Transitional
          Cramped Inner Kampungs  │    Informal: Packed Housing
          + Heat & Canopy Loss)   │    in Greener Urban Margins)
                                  ▼
                 y-: Informal Residential Fabric
    (Small-Footprint Buildings, Tight Packing, Mixed Roofs)
```

Green canopy and housing quality do not always move together: a leafy peri-urban *kampung* still lacks formal housing layout, while a paved downtown business block looks barren and hot without being a slum. Separating built form ($y+$ formal layout with larger, spaced dwellings vs. $y-$ tight, small-footprint housing) from micro-environment ($x+$ vegetated and cooler vs. $x-$ treeless and heat-stressed) captures these four distinct neighborhood types (`Quadrants I–IV`) and explains why both domains must be checked separately before combining them or testing spatial boundaries.
